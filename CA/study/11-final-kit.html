Performance, components and cost

Asked: CPU time in every final, Von Neumann diagram, Amdahl

Performance means one thing: how long a program takes to run. Lecture 1 breaks that time into three factors, and every numerical in this tab is the same equation rearranged.

CPU time = Instruction count × CPI × Clock cycle time = Instruction count × CPI / Clock rate

Once you know which factor a change affects, the question is solved. A better compiler cuts the instruction count; faster circuits shorten the cycle; a cleverer pipeline lowers the CPI.

Exam answers

What is computer architecture, and how does it differ from computer organization (microarchitecture)?17-18 · 4fJun '20 · 1c2m

Computer architecture is the set of attributes a programmer can see: the instruction set, addressing modes, registers and data types. Computer organization, also called the microarchitecture or the implementation, is how those attributes are built in hardware: the datapath, control, pipeline and memory system. For example, x86-64 is one architecture, while Intel's Core and AMD's Ryzen chips are different organizations that implement it.

Draw the components of a computer using the Von Neumann architecture and show how they are connected.20-21 · 2a17-18 · 1d, 1eJun '20 · 1a5m

Draw the left figure below. Then write: the five classic components are input, output, memory, datapath and control; the datapath and control together form the processor (CPU). In a Von Neumann machine a single memory holds both the program and its data (the stored-program concept), and the CPU, memory and I/O devices share one system bus made of address, data and control lines.

The CPU repeats the fetch–decode–execute cycle: the program counter supplies the address of the next instruction, the instruction is fetched from memory over the bus, the control unit decodes it, and the datapath (ALU and registers) executes it, loading or storing data over the same bus. Because instructions and data travel on one path, the CPU cannot fetch an instruction and move data at the same moment; this limit is called the Von Neumann bottleneck.

What is the stored-program concept?17-18 · 1fJun '20 · 1c1–2m

Instructions are represented as numbers and stored in memory alongside the data, so a computer runs a different program simply by loading different numbers into memory rather than by being rewired. It is what makes a computer general-purpose.

Response time vs throughput.new2m

Response time (execution time, latency) is the time between the start and the completion of one task; a desktop user cares about it. Throughput (bandwidth) is the total amount of work done per unit time; a server or datacenter cares about it. A faster processor improves both; adding more processors for independent jobs improves throughput only.

In CPU time = IC × CPI × CCT, what do IC, CPI and CCT mean, and what hardware and software affect each?TT 18-19 · 32m

IC is the instruction count, the number of instructions executed. CPI is the average clock cycles per instruction. CCT is the clock cycle time, the length of one cycle (the inverse of the clock rate).

What unit of measurement is used for the clock period of a processor?20-21 · 1e2m

The clock period is a time, measured in seconds, in practice nanoseconds (1 ns = 10−9 s) or picoseconds (1 ps = 10−12 s). Its inverse, the clock rate, is measured in hertz, usually gigahertz. For example, a 4 GHz clock has a period of 1 / (4 × 109) s = 0.25 ns = 250 ps.

Why is it difficult to use just one metric to analyze the performance of a computer?21-22 · 1e2m

Because execution time depends on three factors that trade off against each other: instruction count, CPI and clock rate. A machine with a faster clock may need more cycles per instruction, and an instruction set with fewer instructions may take longer per instruction, so clock rate, MIPS or CPI alone can mislead. Performance also depends on the workload, and users weigh response time differently from throughput, so the reliable measure is the execution time of real programs, summarised over a benchmark suite such as SPEC.

Computer A runs a program in 10 s and computer B in 15 s. How much faster is A than B?Jun '20 · B1e1m

Performance is the inverse of execution time, so A is 15 / 10 = 1.5 times faster than B. "X is n times faster than Y" means Time(Y) / Time(X) = n.

How is the cost of an integrated circuit determined? What is yield?new15-16 · B4i2–5m

A chip (die) is cut from a silicon wafer. The book's three equations:

Cost per die = Cost per wafer / (Dies per wafer × Yield) Dies per wafer ≈ Wafer area / Die area Yield = 1 / (1 + Defects per area × Die area / 2)²

Yield is the percentage of good dies out of all the dies on the wafer. A defect is a microscopic flaw that can ruin the die it lands on. Cost rises sharply with die area, because a bigger die means fewer dies per wafer and a lower yield. The lecture adds two more factors: high volume spreads the fixed engineering cost over more chips, and the learning curve lowers cost as manufacturing experience grows. Feature size is the minimum transistor or wire dimension of the process; shrinking it fits more transistors on a die.

What is the power wall, and why did clock rates stop rising?new2m

Dynamic power in CMOS chips is

Power = Capacitive load × Voltage² × Frequency switched

For decades the voltage was lowered with every generation, which allowed higher clock rates at similar power. Around 2004 voltages could not drop much further (transistors leak too much), and chips reached the limit of the heat that can be removed, about 100 W. So designers stopped raising the clock rate and put several cores on each chip instead. The book's example: a processor with 85% of the capacitive load running at 15% lower voltage and 15% lower frequency uses 0.85 × 0.85² × 0.85 = 0.854 ≈ 0.52 of the power.

Numericals, solved

P1 runs a program of 2500 instructions at 4 GHz with an IPC of 0.8. How many seconds does it take?20-21 · 2e5m
  1. IPC is instructions per cycle, so CPI = 1 / IPC = 1 / 0.8 = 1.25.
  2. Clock cycles = IC × CPI = 2500 × 1.25 = 3125 cycles.
  3. CPU time = cycles / clock rate = 3125 / (4 × 109) s.
  4. CPU time = 7.8125 × 10−7 s = 781.25 ns.
P1 runs 2500 instructions at 4 GHz with an average CPI of 2. How long does it take?21-22 · 5c5m
  1. Cycles = 2500 × 2 = 5000.
  2. Clock cycle time = 1 / (4 × 109) = 0.25 ns.
  3. CPU time = 5000 × 0.25 ns = 1250 ns = 1.25 µs = 1.25 × 10−6 s.
1 billion instructions at 5 GHz: 40% take 3 cycles, 50% take 4, 10% take 5. Execution time?21-22 · 2f17-18 · 6a5m

  2. Cycles = 109 × 3.7 = 3.7 × 109.
  3. Time = 3.7 × 109 / (5 × 109) = 0.74 s.

The 15-16 version (4 GHz; 20% at 3, 30% at 4, 40% at 4, 10% at 5 cycles): CPI = 0.6 + 1.2 + 1.6 + 0.5 = 3.9, time = 3.9 × 109 / 4 × 109 = 0.975 s.

ALU 50% at 1 cycle, loads 20% at 5, stores 10% at 1, branches 20% at 2. Which improves performance more: (A) branch prediction cuts branches to 1 cycle, or (B) faster memory cuts loads to 3 cycles?21-22 · 6b10m
  1. Original CPI = 0.5 × 1 + 0.2 × 5 + 0.1 × 1 + 0.2 × 2 = 0.5 + 1.0 + 0.1 + 0.4 = 2.0.
  2. (A) branches at 1 cycle: CPI = 0.5 + 1.0 + 0.1 + 0.2 = 1.8. Speedup = 2.0 / 1.8 = 1.11.
  3. (B) loads at 3 cycles: CPI = 0.5 + 0.6 + 0.1 + 0.4 = 1.6. Speedup = 2.0 / 1.6 = 1.25.

  5. Option B (faster loads) improves performance more: 25% against 11%.

Derive Amdahl's speedup formula. A web server's new processor is 10 times faster at computation; the old one spent 40% of its time computing and 60% on I/O. Overall speedup?20-21 · 2dTT 18-19 · 45m

Let the old time be T, with a fraction F improved by a factor k. New time = (1 − F)T + FT / k, so

Speedup = T / T_new = 1 / ((1 − F) + F / k)
  1. F = 0.4, k = 10.
  2. Speedup = 1 / (0.6 + 0.4 / 10) = 1 / 0.64.
  3. Speedup = 1.5625, far below 10 because the I/O time is unchanged.

With 30% computation (TT 18-19): 1 / (0.7 + 0.03) = 1.37. More Amdahl questions are in the L9 tab.

Traps

  • IPC is the inverse of CPI. An IPC of 0.8 is a CPI of 1.25, not 0.8.
  • GHz to cycle time: 1 / (4 × 109) s = 0.25 ns. Keep the powers of ten visible in your working.
  • "How much faster": divide the slower time by the faster time, so the answer is above 1.
  • Percentages in a mix are of instructions; multiply each by its CPI before adding.

Doomsday

  1. CPU time = IC × CPI / clock rate. CPI = 1 / IPC.
  2. Mix CPI = Σ (fraction × cycles). Speedup = old time / new time.
  3. Five components: input, output, memory, datapath, control. Datapath + control = processor.
  4. Von Neumann: one memory and bus for code and data (bottleneck). Harvard: separate memories and buses.
  5. Amdahl: 1 / ((1 − F) + F / k).

Instruction set architecture

Asked: addressing modes in both 2024 finals, RISC vs CISC, PC, ALU, stack and heap

The instruction set architecture (ISA) is the contract between software and hardware. It lists everything a machine-language program can rely on: the instructions, the registers, and how an instruction finds its operands. Lecture 2 sorts ISAs by where operands live (stack, accumulator, register-memory, load-store), lists the ways an instruction can locate an operand (addressing modes), and compares the two design philosophies, RISC and CISC.

Exam answers

What is an ISA? What is an implementation of an ISA? Name some common ISAs.20-21 · 4b17-18 · 4aJun '20 · B1a2m

The instruction set architecture is the abstract interface between the hardware and the lowest-level software: the instructions, registers, memory access and I/O a machine-language program needs to run correctly. An implementation is hardware that obeys that abstraction (the book's definition). Many implementations can share one ISA at different cost and speed; Intel and AMD both implement x86-64. Common ISAs: x86 / x86-64, ARM, MIPS, RISC-V, PowerPC, SPARC.

What are an instruction, an instruction set and an opcode?17-18 · 4dJun '20 · B1b, 1c1–2m

An instruction is a single command the processor understands, stored as a binary word. The instruction set is the whole vocabulary of instructions a given architecture understands. The opcode is the field of an instruction that says which operation it performs (and its format), such as the 6-bit op field of a MIPS instruction.

Show the stack and accumulator architectures with figures, and write code for A = B × C + D on each.TT 18-19 · 1on slide6m

ISAs are classified by where an instruction's operands are stored. In a stack machine both operands are implicit: they are the top two entries of a stack. In an accumulator machine one operand is implicitly the accumulator register and the other is a memory address. A register-memory machine names one register and one memory operand (x86). A load-store (register-register) machine does arithmetic only on registers and touches memory only with loads and stores (MIPS, ARM, RISC-V).

Why do registers matter? What is register pressure?21-22 · 4b2m

Registers are much faster than memory and there are few of them, so a compiler can keep the most-used values there. MIPS has 32 general-purpose 32-bit registers; $zero is always 0, $s0–$s7 hold variables, $t0–$t9 hold temporaries, $sp is the stack pointer and $ra the return address. Register pressure is the demand for registers compared with how many exist. When a program has more live values than registers, the compiler must spill some of them to memory (the stack) and reload them later, which adds loads and stores and slows the program.

Choice (21-22 Q2d): RISC. Simple fixed-length instructions with few formats and addressing modes need a small, hardwired decoder and no microcode ROM, so the control logic uses far fewer transistors. With only loads and stores touching memory, the datapath is regular and easy to pipeline, and the saved area and power can go to registers and caches. The cost is more instructions per program, which a good compiler offsets.

Modern x86 chips blend both: they decode CISC instructions into RISC-like micro-operations internally (slide).

Compare the x86 and ARM architectures.new2–5m

x86 began with the Intel 8086 in 1978. It is CISC, became the desktop and server standard, and was extended to 64 bits (x86-64). Today's x86 chips have deep pipelines and translate each x86 instruction into RISC-like micro-operations. ARM (Acorn RISC Machine, 1985) was RISC from the start and designed for low power; it powers most phones and tablets, and ARMv8-A added a 64-bit mode (AArch64). The trade-off: x86 stresses backward compatibility and single-thread speed, while ARM stresses energy efficiency and simplicity, the same RISC vs CISC split now seen between mobile and desktop.

What is the purpose of the program counter (PC)?20-21 · 1aTT1 '26 · g2m

The PC is a register that holds the address of the next instruction to be fetched. Each cycle the instruction at that address is read from memory, and the PC is normally incremented to the next instruction (by 4 in MIPS, since instructions are 4 bytes). Branches and jumps change the flow of the program by loading a new target address into the PC, and a procedure call saves PC + 4 in $ra so the program can return.

Describe the purpose of an ALU.20-21 · 4a21-22 · 4d2m

The arithmetic logic unit is the part of the datapath that performs the arithmetic (add, subtract) and logical (AND, OR, NOR, shifts, comparisons such as set-on-less-than) operations on its inputs. Besides arithmetic instructions, it computes memory addresses for loads and stores (base + offset) and compares operands for branches (subtract and test for zero). It also produces status signals such as zero and overflow.

What types of information are contained in stack memory?20-21 · 5b5m

Each procedure call pushes a procedure frame (activation record) onto the stack. It contains:

  • the return address ($ra), saved when the procedure itself calls another procedure;
  • saved registers: the $s registers the procedure must preserve for its caller, and any $t or argument registers the caller wants kept;
  • arguments beyond the first four (MIPS passes four in $a0–$a3);
  • local variables that do not fit in registers, such as local arrays and structures;
  • the old frame pointer ($fp), which marks the start of the frame.

The stack pointer $sp points to the top of the stack. Pushing subtracts from $sp and popping adds to it, because the MIPS stack grows from high addresses to low.

What is a stack overflow, and how does it occur?20-21 · 4cTT1 '25 · 1c2m

A stack overflow happens when the stack grows beyond the memory set aside for it. Every call pushes a frame, so very deep or infinite recursion, or a procedure with very large local arrays, keeps pushing until the stack runs into the heap or a guard region. The program then crashes (a segmentation fault) or overwrites other data.

Memory hierarchy, caches and virtual memory

The biggest block of marks: a 10-mark trace in every recent final, write policies, VM

Programs reuse what they touched recently (temporal locality) and touch what is near it (spatial locality). So a small, fast memory next to the processor that keeps recently used blocks answers most requests, and the machine feels as fast as the small memory while being as big as the large one.

A cache is a small table. Part of the address, the index, picks a row; the tag stored in that row says which memory block is really there; the offset picks the byte inside the block. Virtual memory is the same idea one level down: pages instead of blocks, disk instead of DRAM, and a page table instead of tags.

Explain how the principle of locality increases the performance of a computer.15-16 · 6b4m

Because programs touch only a small part of their address space at any moment, a small, fast memory near the processor can hold that working set. Recently used items stay in the cache (temporal locality) and whole blocks are fetched so neighbours arrive too (spatial locality). Most accesses then hit in the fast level, often over 95% of them, so the average access time is close to the fast memory's while the capacity is that of the large, cheap memory. Average memory access time = hit time + miss rate × miss penalty, and locality keeps the miss rate small.

What is the hit rate of a cache? Define miss rate, hit time and miss penalty. How do they affect AMAT?20-21 · 1d15-16 · 2e2m

Hit rate is the fraction of memory accesses found in the cache. Miss rate = 1 − hit rate. Hit time is the time to access the cache, including the time to decide hit or miss. Miss penalty is the extra time to fetch the block from the lower level, insert it and pass it to the processor.

AMAT = Hit time + Miss rate × Miss penalty Example (slide): 1 + 0.05 × 20 = 2 cycles

AMAT rises linearly with each of the three, so a cache can be improved by lowering any of them.

Cache structure and placement

What is block placement? What are the possible ways to do it?21-22 · 4f2m

Block placement decides where in the cache a memory block may be put. There are three ways. Direct mapped: exactly one place, (block address) mod (number of blocks). Set associative: any of the n places in one set, where the set is (block address) mod (number of sets). Fully associative: anywhere in the cache. Example from the book: memory block 12 in an 8-block cache goes to block 4 if direct mapped (12 mod 8), to set 0 if 2-way set associative (12 mod 4), and anywhere if fully associative.

What is a set-associative cache?21-22 · 4e2m

A cache divided into sets of n blocks (n ways). Each memory block maps to exactly one set, (block address) mod (number of sets), but may be stored in any of the n blocks of that set. On an access, the index selects the set and all n tags in it are compared in parallel. A direct-mapped cache is 1-way set associative; a fully associative cache is a single set holding every block.

If a cache is n-way set associative with m sets, what is n when it is direct mapped, and when it is fully associative?20-21 · 1h2m

The cache holds n × m blocks. Direct mapped: n = 1 (one block per set, and m equals the number of blocks). Fully associative: n = the total number of blocks in the cache, with m = 1 (one set).

What advantages does fully associative mapping offer compared with direct mapping? Which is better for hit ratio and for search speed?TT2 '25 · 1b15-16 · B4n2m

In a fully associative cache a block can go anywhere, so there are no conflict misses: two busy blocks never evict each other just because they share an index. This gives the highest hit ratio for a given size, and the cache is free to choose which block to replace (for example the least recently used one). The cost is search speed and hardware: every tag must be compared, which needs one comparator per block, is slower and uses more power. So fully associative wins on hit ratio; direct mapped wins on search speed (one comparison) and simplicity.

Explain how data is located in the cache (block identification).15-16 · 6a, B4m1–4m

Compare the effect of a larger block size and a bigger cache on the miss rate.15-16 · 5c5m

A larger block exploits spatial locality, so it reduces compulsory misses. But in a cache of fixed size it means fewer blocks, so blocks compete more and conflict and capacity misses can rise once blocks become a large fraction of the cache; it also raises the miss penalty, since more bytes must be transferred. A bigger cache reduces capacity (and some conflict) misses without raising the miss penalty, but it costs more area and power and lengthens the hit time. Larger blocks help most when memory bandwidth is high; a bigger cache helps when the working set is larger than the cache.

Discuss the metrics for cache optimization.21-22 · 1h2m

Why do we combine write-through with no-write-allocate?21-22 · 5f5m

With write-through, every write goes to memory anyway, so loading the block into the cache on a write miss saves nothing for that write. It would only cost a block fetch from memory and might evict useful data, and it pays off only if the block is read soon afterwards. So write-through caches usually skip the allocation: the write goes straight to memory (through the write buffer) and the cache is used for reads. Programs that write whole blocks, such as the operating system zeroing a page, make allocation especially wasteful.

Write-back is the opposite case. Allocating the block lets later writes to it be absorbed by the cache and written to memory once, at eviction, so write-back pairs with write-allocate.

What is the effect of a write buffer? How can a merging write buffer reduce the miss penalty? What happens in no-write-allocate?15-16 · 5a, 6b, B4e1–4m

A write buffer is a small queue between the cache and memory. In a write-through cache the processor puts the store in the buffer and continues at once; the buffer drains to memory in the background, so the processor stalls only when the buffer is full. A merging write buffer checks whether a new write is to a block already waiting in the buffer and, if so, combines them into one entry. The buffer fills more slowly, so there are fewer stalls, and several words go to memory in one multiword transfer, which uses memory bandwidth better. In no-write-allocate, a write miss updates only the lower level; the block is not brought into the cache.

State the difference between separate and unified caches.21-22 · 4a2m

A separate (split) design has one cache for instructions and another for data. An instruction fetch and a data access can happen in the same cycle, which a pipeline needs, and each cache can be tuned for its own use. A unified cache holds both instructions and data. For the same total size it has a slightly lower miss rate, since space is shared flexibly, but its single port forces an instruction fetch and a load or store to take turns. Split L1 caches with a unified L2 is the usual design.

How do multilevel caches reduce the miss penalty?new2–5m

A second, larger cache (L2) sits between the small, fast L1 and main memory. Most L1 misses are caught by L2, so they cost the L2 access time instead of a full trip to DRAM (100+ cycles). L1 stays small to keep its hit time short; L2 is larger and more associative to keep its miss rate low.

AMAT = Hit time(L1) + Miss rate(L1) × Miss penalty(L1) Miss penalty(L1) = Hit time(L2) + Miss rate(L2) × Miss penalty(L2)

Book example: 4 GHz, base CPI 1, 100 ns memory (400 cycles), 2% L1 misses per instruction. L1 only: CPI = 1 + 0.02 × 400 = 9. Adding a 5 ns (20-cycle) L2 that leaves 0.5% going to memory: CPI = 1 + 0.02 × 20 + 0.005 × 400 = 3.4, which is 2.6 times faster.

Give a scheme to decide which block to replace in a fully associative cache.20-21 · 4d2m

Least recently used (LRU): evict the block that has gone unused longest; it exploits temporal locality. Others: FIFO (evict the block resident longest), random (simple hardware, surprisingly good), and optimal (Belady's), which evicts the block used furthest in the future and is only a theoretical benchmark. Replacement only matters for associative caches; a direct-mapped cache has no choice. True LRU gets expensive beyond 4–8 ways, so hardware approximates it.

What is the critical word first mechanism (and early restart)?15-16 · 2c-ii2m

Both cut the miss penalty by not waiting for the whole block. Early restart: the processor resumes as soon as the requested word arrives, while the rest of the block is still being filled. Critical word first: memory sends the requested word first, then the remaining words, wrapping around to the start of the block, so the processor can restart even sooner.

Virtual memory

What is virtual memory, and what are its advantages?17-18 · 1bJun '20 · 1a, 1b1–2m

Virtual memory uses main memory as a "cache" for secondary storage. Each program gets its own virtual address space, divided into pages, and hardware with the OS translates virtual addresses to physical addresses through a page table. Advantages: relocation (a program can be loaded anywhere in physical memory without changing its code), protection (each process's page table isolates it from the others), safe sharing of memory among programs, and the illusion of a memory larger than physical DRAM, backed by disk.

What does a page table entry contain? What is a page fault, and how is it handled?15-16 · 5eslide2–5m

The virtual address is split into a virtual page number (VPN) and a page offset. The page table, one per process, is indexed by the VPN. Each entry holds:

  • Valid bit: is the page in physical memory? (0 means a page fault.)
  • Physical page number (PPN): replaces the VPN; the offset is unchanged.
  • Dirty bit: the page was written, so it must be saved to disk before eviction.
  • Reference (use) bit: set on access, used to approximate LRU.
  • Protection bits: read, write and execute permissions.

A page fault means the page is on disk, not in memory. The OS takes over: it picks a physical frame (evicting a page, and writing it back if dirty), reads the page in from disk, updates the page table and restarts the instruction. This takes millions of cycles, so page faults must be very rare.

For "explain cache hit, cache miss, page fault and page table" (15-16 Q5e): a hit finds the block in the cache; a miss fetches it from the next level and stalls the pipeline; a page fault is a "miss" in main memory, handled in software by the OS; the page table is the per-process map from virtual to physical page numbers.

  2. Physical address = log2(4 GB) = 32 bits, so PPN = 32 − 16 = 16 bits.
  3. VPN = 48 − 16 = 32 bits, so the page table has 232 = 4,294,967,296 entries.
  4. With 4-byte entries that is 234 bytes = 16 GB per process, more than the physical memory. This is why multi-level page tables are needed (part iii).
What are the advantages of a TLB? Describe how a TLB works using a diagram.20-21 · 6c-ii3m

Without help, every memory access would need two memory accesses: one to read the page table entry and one for the data. The translation lookaside buffer (TLB) is a small cache of recent translations, typically 16–512 entries and fully or highly associative. Its tag is the VPN; its data is the PPN with the valid, dirty, reference and protection bits.

  1. The VPN of the virtual address is looked up in the TLB.
  2. TLB hit: the PPN comes out at once and is joined to the unchanged page offset to form the physical address, which goes to the cache. No page-table access is needed.
  3. TLB miss: the page table in memory is read. If the entry is valid, it is loaded into the TLB and the access retried. If it is invalid, it is a page fault and the OS loads the page from disk.

Advantages: address translation takes about one cycle instead of a memory access; most accesses hit because of locality (a TLB miss rate of 0.01–1%); the protection checks happen on the same lookup.

What is a multi-level page table, and why is it necessary in modern systems?20-21 · 6c-iii3m

A multi-level page table is a tree of small tables. The VPN is split into fields: the first field indexes a first-level table, whose entry points to a second-level table, indexed by the next field, and so on; the last level holds the PPN. Second-level tables are allocated only for the parts of the address space a program actually uses. It is necessary because a flat table for a large address space is enormous: with 48-bit addresses and 64 KB pages, the flat table needs 232 entries (16 GB per process at 4 bytes each), even though most of a program's address space is empty. The cost is several memory reads per translation on a TLB miss, which the TLB hides most of the time.

Given 4 KB pages, how many bytes can a 16-way set-associative VIPT cache hold?20-21 · 4h2m

A virtually indexed, physically tagged cache reads its index from the page offset, which needs no translation, so the index and block offset together must fit in the 12-bit offset of a 4 KB page. Each way can therefore be at most one page (4 KB), and the cache can hold 16 × 4 KB = 64 KB.

Asked: IEEE 754 in every recent final and both term-test years; two's complement; endianness

A bit pattern means nothing until a type is assigned to it: the same 32 bits can be an integer, a float, an instruction or four characters. Integers use two's complement, where the top bit has weight −2n−1. Real numbers use IEEE 754: a sign, a biased exponent, and a fraction with a hidden leading 1, just like scientific notation in base 2.

value = (−1)^S × (1 + Fraction) × 2^(Exponent − Bias) Bias = 127 (single), 1023 (double)

Differentiate between big-endian and little-endian systems using a diagram.21-22 · 2bTT1 '26 · h2–5m

Endianness is the order in which the bytes of a multi-byte word are stored in byte-addressed memory. Big-endian stores the most significant byte at the lowest address (the "big end" first); MIPS, IBM mainframes and network protocols use it. Little-endian stores the least significant byte at the lowest address; x86 uses it. The word's value is the same either way; only the byte order in memory differs, which matters when data moves between machines or is read byte by byte.

Bit, byte, word and data type.slide2m

A bit is a single 0 or 1. A byte is 8 bits, the smallest addressable unit of memory on most machines. A word is the natural unit a processor operates on: 32 bits in MIPS, often 64 bits today. A data type is the interpretation applied to a bit pattern (integer, float, character, address); the hardware or program decides it, not the bits.

When does overflow occur in two's complement, and how is it detected?slide2m

Overflow happens when the true result does not fit in the bit width. In addition it can only happen when both operands have the same sign, and it shows as a result with the opposite sign: two positives giving a negative, or two negatives giving a positive. In subtraction A − B it happens when A and B have different signs and the result's sign differs from A's. A carry out of the top bit is not by itself overflow; equivalently, overflow occurs when the carry into the sign bit differs from the carry out of it.

ASCII, Unicode and UTF-8.slide2m

ASCII is a 7-bit code with 128 values for letters, digits, punctuation and control codes: 'A' = 65, 'a' = 97 (cases differ by exactly 32), '0' = 48 (so a digit character minus 48 is its value), space = 32. UTF-8 encodes Unicode in 1 to 4 bytes per character and is backward-compatible with ASCII. C strings are bytes ending with a null byte (0).

Pipelining, hazards and instruction-level parallelism

Asked: a 10-mark hazard or timing-diagram question in both 2024 finals, forwarding, branch prediction, superscalar

A pipeline is an assembly line. Each instruction passes through five stages: IF fetch, ID decode and read registers, EX execute in the ALU, MEM access data memory, WB write the result back. Five instructions are in flight at once, one per stage. Each instruction still takes 5 cycles (latency is unchanged), but one finishes every cycle (throughput rises up to five times).

Hazards break the flow. A data hazard is an instruction needing a result that is not written yet; forwarding passes the result straight from a pipeline register, and a stall waits when even that is too late (a load followed by its use). A control hazard is not knowing which instruction follows a branch; the pipeline predicts and flushes when wrong. Lecture 8 goes further: issue several instructions per cycle (instruction-level parallelism).

The pipeline

What are pipeline stages? Briefly explain the classic five-stage pipeline of a RISC processor, including the purpose of the IF, MEM and WB cycles.20-21 · 4e15-16 · 6c, 3c2–6m

Pipeline stages are the steps an instruction's execution is divided into, each done by its own hardware in one clock cycle, separated by pipeline registers so that different instructions can occupy different stages at once.

StageWhat happens
IF instruction fetchread the instruction at address PC from instruction memory; PC ← PC + 4

Once the pipeline is full, one instruction completes every cycle, so for many instructions the speedup approaches the number of stages, provided the stages are balanced and there are no hazards.

Why is a single-cycle implementation not used today? What are the advantages of a pipelined datapath?Jun '20 · 2b2.5m

In a single-cycle design every instruction takes one clock cycle, so the clock period must fit the slowest instruction (a load, which uses every unit). Faster instructions waste most of that long cycle, and each hardware unit sits idle most of the time. A pipelined datapath splits execution into stages with a short clock period set by the slowest stage, and overlaps instructions so all units are busy, which raises throughput nearly in proportion to the number of stages while reusing the same hardware.

True or false: "Pipelining reduces the instruction time of an individual instruction." "Data hazard arises because of dependencies on consecutive instructions."15-16 · 1n, 1o1m each

False. Pipelining improves throughput; each instruction's latency stays the same or grows slightly because of pipeline-register delay. True. A data hazard occurs when an instruction depends on the result of an earlier instruction that is still in the pipeline, which happens for nearby, usually consecutive, instructions.

Describe how operand forwarding works. When is a stall still needed?20-21 · 5e5m

An ALU result exists at the end of EX, but it is written to the register file only in WB, two cycles later. Forwarding (bypassing) adds paths from the pipeline registers back to the ALU inputs, so a dependent instruction gets the value as soon as it exists instead of waiting for WB. A forwarding unit compares the source registers of the instruction in EX with the destination registers of the instructions in the EX/MEM and MEM/WB registers; on a match (and a non-zero destination register), multiplexers select the forwarded value instead of the register-file value.

  • EX/MEM → EX: from the instruction just ahead (distance 1).
  • MEM/WB → EX: from the instruction two ahead, or from a load.

Forwarding costs no cycles, but it cannot send a value back in time. A load followed immediately by an instruction that uses the loaded register (a load-use hazard) needs one stall: the data comes out of memory at the end of MEM, when the next instruction is already in EX. The hazard-detection unit inserts one bubble, then the value is forwarded from MEM/WB. The compiler can avoid the stall by reordering code.

Branches

What is a control hazard? Describe the common methods to overcome it.Jun '20 · 2a17-18 · 2d15-16 · 3c2.5–5m

A control (branch) hazard arises because the pipeline must fetch the next instruction before it knows whether a branch is taken. Methods:

  1. Stall until the branch is resolved. Simple, but costs cycles on every branch.
  2. Decide the branch earlier: move the comparison and target calculation into ID, so a wrong guess costs one cycle instead of two.
  3. Static prediction: predict not taken (keep fetching in sequence and flush on a taken branch), or predict backward branches taken (loops).
  4. Dynamic prediction: a branch prediction buffer records recent outcomes per branch (1-bit or 2-bit) and guesses from history; with deep pipelines this is essential.
  5. Delayed branch: the instruction right after the branch (the delay slot) always executes, and the compiler fills it with a useful independent instruction.

Static prediction is useful because it needs no hardware tables and is right often enough (loops branch backward most of the time), which removes most of the stall cost of a simple pipeline.

The 2-bit predictor: each branch has a 2-bit counter in a branch prediction buffer, indexed by the low bits of the branch address. The prediction changes only after two wrong guesses in a row, so a loop branch that is taken many times and not taken once at exit is mispredicted only once per loop (a 1-bit predictor would miss twice). Discuss the issues with longer-latency pipelines, with examples, and how they are handled.21-22 · 5d5m

Floating-point operations take several cycles in EX (for example a 4-stage FP adder, a 7-stage multiplier and an unpipelined divider), so the pipeline has units of different lengths. Issues:

  • Structural hazards: an unpipelined unit such as the divider cannot accept a new instruction until it finishes, and several instructions may want to write the register file in the same cycle.
  • Longer RAW stalls: an instruction using a multiply result must wait many cycles even with forwarding.
  • WAW hazards: a short instruction can finish before a longer earlier one that writes the same register.
  • Out-of-order completion makes exceptions imprecise: a later instruction may have written its result when an earlier one faults.
  • Deeper pipelines also raise the branch misprediction penalty.

Handling: detect hazards in ID and stall the issue until the unit and the operands are free; track which registers have pending writes (a scoreboard); stall or cancel to prevent WAW; arbitrate the register-file write port; buffer results and commit in order (a reorder buffer) for precise exceptions; use dynamic branch prediction. Example: MUL.D F0,F4,F6 then ADD.D F2,F0,F8 stalls ADD.D until the multiply's last stage forwards F0.

Describe superscalar processing.20-21 · 4f2m

A superscalar processor issues more than one instruction per clock cycle, chosen by the hardware at run time (dynamic multiple issue). It has several functional units and checks dependences as it fetches, so it can reach an IPC above 1 (a CPI below 1). Most superscalars also schedule dynamically, executing instructions out of order around stalls while committing results in order.

Explain out-of-order execution: reservation stations, register renaming, reorder buffer, speculation.15-16 · B4lslide2–5m

Out-of-order execution: instructions execute as soon as their operands are ready, not in program order, so independent work continues past a stalled instruction (such as one waiting on a cache miss).

  • Reservation stations buffer each instruction and its operands until all are ready, then send it to a functional unit.
  • Register renaming: each result gets its own physical location (the reservation station or a physical register), which removes false WAR and WAW dependences.
  • Reorder buffer and commit: results are held until it is safe to write them in the original program order, so exceptions stay precise.
  • Speculation: guess past an unresolved branch and execute ahead; because nothing commits until the guess is confirmed, wrong-path results are simply discarded.

The slide's point: dynamic scheduling hides stalls a compiler cannot predict, and lets the processor speculate safely.

Loop unrolling: unroll for (I = 1; I <= N; I++) A[I] = A[I] * 1.1; by a factor of 5.SWE 18-19 · B2aTT2 18-19 · 32m
for (I = 1; I + 4 <= N; I += 5) { A[I] = A[I] * 1.1; A[I+1] = A[I+1] * 1.1; A[I+2] = A[I+2] * 1.1; A[I+3] = A[I+3] * 1.1; A[I+4] = A[I+4] * 1.1; } for (; I <= N; I++) /* leftover N mod 5 iterations */ A[I] = A[I] * 1.1;

Unrolling removes four out of five loop-control instructions (increment, compare, branch) and gives the scheduler five independent multiplies to interleave, hiding their latency. Costs: larger code and more registers (register pressure).

Explain what SIMD means and why GPUs or AI coprocessors primarily use SIMD.TT2 '26 · 25m

SIMD (single instruction, multiple data): one control unit fetches and decodes an instruction once and sends it to many processing units, each applying it to its own data element at the same time. Why GPUs and AI chips use it:

  1. Their work is massively data-parallel: the same shader on millions of independent pixels, the same multiply-add on millions of tensor elements.
  2. One control unit drives many ALUs, so fetch, decode and hazard checks are paid once per instruction ("amortize the cost of the control unit"); most transistors and power go to arithmetic, giving far more throughput per watt, and power is the main limit.
  3. Less instruction bandwidth and smaller code: one instruction does a whole loop's work.
  4. Regular memory access: neighbouring lanes read neighbouring addresses, merged into wide transfers from high-bandwidth memory.
  5. SIMD's weakness, branchy code, rarely matters in these kernels, and GPUs hide memory delay with many threads instead of large caches.
Why does vector processing increase the performance of a processor?20-21 · 5f5m
  1. One vector instruction does the work of a whole loop, so instruction fetch and decode bandwidth drops dramatically.
  2. The elements of a vector are declared independent, so there are no data-hazard checks inside a vector instruction.
  3. Hazards between vector instructions are checked once per vector, not per element.
  4. The memory access pattern is known, so interleaved memory banks deliver the vector and memory latency is paid once per vector, not once per word.
  5. The loop branch disappears, so there are no control hazards.
  6. Fewer instructions and checks save power and energy.

Book example, DAXPY (Y = a × X + Y, 64 elements): about 600 MIPS instructions (2 + 9 × 64 = 578) against 6 vector instructions, and about 64 times fewer pipeline stalls.

Amdahl's law for parallel processors

80% of the work can be parallelized. How many processing units give a speedup of 4, and what is the speedup for a single unit?20-21 · 5c5m

  2. 4 = 1 / (0.2 + 0.8 / N), so 0.2 + 0.8 / N = 0.25, 0.8 / N = 0.05.
  3. N = 16 processing units. With one unit, speedup = 1 / (0.2 + 0.8) = 1.
  4. Add: the maximum with infinitely many units is 1 / 0.2 = 5.

How do you apply Amdahl's law to parallel processing? "A large number of processors gives only limited speedup for a single program." Agree?TT 18-19 · 54m

Take the improved part as the parallel fraction F and the improvement factor as the number of processors N: Speedup = 1 / ((1 − F) + F / N). Agree: as N grows, F / N → 0 and the speedup approaches 1 / (1 − F), set by the sequential part alone. A 90% parallel program gets 9.17 on 100 processors and only 9.91 on 1000. Communication, synchronization and load imbalance lower it further. The limit holds for a fixed problem size (strong scaling); when the problem grows with the machine (weak scaling), large machines can still be used well.

Book examples: 90× on 100 processors; the 10 × 10 vs 100 × 100 matrix sum; load balancing; strong vs weak scaling.§7.25m

  • Strong scaling: speedup with the problem size fixed. Weak scaling: the problem grows in proportion to the number of processors.

Input/output: buses, interrupts, DMA, RAID

Asked: buses (three times), interrupts, memory-mapped I/O. RAID, polling and DMA are on the slides but not yet asked.

I/O is designed the other way round from processors: dependability and cost come first, performance second, because losing data is worse than losing time. Devices are connected by buses or point-to-point links. They get the processor's attention by polling (the processor keeps checking) or interrupts (the device signals), and bulk transfers go through DMA without the processor copying every word. RAID spends extra disks to survive a disk failure.

What are interrupts?20-21 · 4g2m

An interrupt is a signal from an I/O device (an exception that comes from outside the processor) asking for attention. The processor finishes the current instruction, saves the address of the interrupted instruction (in MIPS, in the EPC register) and the cause, and jumps to an interrupt handler in the operating system. After servicing the device, it restores the state and resumes the interrupted program. Interrupts let the processor do useful work instead of waiting for slow devices. An exception in general is any unscheduled event that disrupts execution; internal ones such as arithmetic overflow or an undefined instruction are exceptions, external ones from devices are interrupts.

What is DMA?slide2m

Direct memory access lets a device transfer data to or from memory without the processor moving each word. A DMA controller is given the memory address, the number of bytes and the direction; it then performs the transfer on its own and interrupts the processor only when the transfer finishes or an error occurs. This frees the processor during large transfers such as disk blocks.

Define memory-mapped I/O.21-22 · 1g2m

In memory-mapped I/O, portions of the address space are assigned to I/O devices, and reads and writes to those addresses are interpreted as commands or data for the device (the book's definition). Ordinary load and store instructions therefore do I/O, and the memory system ignores those addresses. The alternative is special I/O instructions (such as x86 in and out) with a separate I/O address space.

Dependability: MTTF, MTTR, availability, FIT. Why is MTTF a reliability measure? MTTF for 107 FIT?15-16 · 5b, 6b, 1f2–4m

Reliability is continuous service accomplishment from a reference point, and MTTF (mean time to failure) measures exactly that: how long a module runs before it fails, so it is a reliability measure. Service interruption is measured by MTTR (mean time to repair). MTBF = MTTF + MTTR.

What are the key properties of flash memory?15-16 · 2b2m

Flash is a nonvolatile semiconductor memory (a kind of EEPROM): it keeps data without power. It is much faster than disk but slower and cheaper per bit than DRAM, uses little power and has no moving parts, so it is shock-resistant. Data is erased in blocks, and each block wears out after about 100,000 write cycles, so controllers use wear leveling to spread writes across blocks. NAND flash is denser and cheaper (storage); NOR flash allows random word access (code).

  • Interrupts come from outside the processor; exceptions such as overflow come from inside.
  • RAID 0 has no redundancy. RAID 5 differs from RAID 4 only in where the parity lives.

Why is energy a better metric than power? What causes static power? What is feature size?15-16 · B4d, B4h, B4c1m each

Energy is tied to a specific task: it decides battery life and the electricity bill. A processor drawing more power can still use less energy if it finishes sooner, so power alone can mislead. Static power comes from leakage current, which flows even when a transistor is off; it grows with the number of transistors and as transistors shrink. Feature size is the minimum size of a transistor or wire in a manufacturing process (for example 45 nm).

What are the two states of a service with respect to an SLA, and how and when do transitions happen?15-16 · 3b4m

Under a service level agreement the service is either in service accomplishment, where it is delivered as specified, or in service interruption, where what is delivered differs from the SLA. A failure moves the service from accomplishment to interruption; a restoration (repair) moves it back. MTTF measures the time spent in accomplishment, MTTR the time in interruption.

Explain availability, scalability and throughput for servers versus desktop computers. Characteristics of an embedded computer?15-16 · 2d, 2a2–5m

Hit under miss (hit-under-3-misses), way prediction, DRAM PRE and ACT commands, bandwidth vs latency trends.15-16 · 2e, 5f, 3a, 5d2–5m
  • Hit under miss: a non-blocking cache keeps serving hits while misses are outstanding; "hit under 3 misses" means it still serves hits with up to three misses pending, which hides miss penalty in out-of-order processors.
  • Way prediction: extra bits per set predict which way the next access will hit, so the multiplexer is set early and only one tag is compared first. A correct guess gives direct-mapped hit time; a wrong guess costs an extra cycle.
  • ACT (activate) opens a DRAM row: the whole row is read into the row buffer, and column reads and writes are then served from it. PRE (precharge) closes the open row and precharges the bit lines so another row can be activated.
  • Trends: for processors, memory, networks and disks, bandwidth has improved far faster than latency (roughly, bandwidth grows at least as the square of the latency improvement). Processor speed grew fastest and DRAM and disk latency slowest, which is why caches matter more and more (the memory wall).
SW R3,100(R0) · LW R1,512(R0) · LW R2,100(R0), all cache index 0. Direct-mapped, write-through, 4-word write buffer not checked on a read miss. Will R2 always equal R3?SWE 18-19 · A2b3m

No. The store writes the cache block and puts the value of R3 into the write buffer. The load of 512 maps to the same index, misses and replaces the block holding 100. The load of 100 then misses and reads main memory, but the buffered write may not have reached memory yet, so R2 can receive the old value: a read-after-write hazard through memory. Fix it by checking the write buffer on a read miss (forwarding from it), or by waiting until the buffer drains before the read.

Assumptions: full forwarding; an FP result is ready at the end of its last EX stage; a load's data at the end of MEM; a store needs its data by MEM; only one instruction uses MEM per cycle.

InstructionStages (cycle numbers)

FMUL F0IF 2, ID 3, stall 4 (waits for F4), M1–M7 5–11, MEM 12, WB 13

STOR F3IF 5, waits; F3 is ready after cycle 15, but MEM 16 is taken by FADD, so MEM 17

Everything has finished after cycle 17 (cycle 18 if you draw a WB stage for the store).

The SWE version has a 6-stage multiplier: subtract one cycle, so the store reaches MEM in cycle 16, and the stored value is (X × A) − A = A(X − 1). Unpipelined divider: it can hold only one division at a time, so a second divide must wait until the first finishes (a structural hazard), stalling the pipeline and cutting throughput.

Hennessy & Patterson's standard example, with latencies FP ALU → FP ALU 3, FP ALU → store 2, load → FP ALU 1, and 1 cycle between the integer decrement and the branch:

Loop: L.D F0, 0(R1) ; load element SUB.D F4, F0, F2 ; F2 holds the constant S.D F4, 0(R1) DADDUI R1, R1, #-8 BNE R1, R2, Loop
  • Unscheduled: 1 stall after L.D, 2 after SUB.D, 1 after DADDUI = 4 stalls, 9 cycles per element.
  • Scheduled (move DADDUI up, adjust the S.D offset to 8): 7 cycles per element.
  • Unrolled 4 times (four L.D/SUB.D/S.D groups using F0/F4, F6/F8, F10/F12, F14/F16, one DADDUI by −32, one BNE): 14 instructions; unscheduled it takes 27 cycles (6.75 per element).
  • Unrolled and scheduled (all four loads, then four subtracts, then the stores with the DADDUI between them): 14 cycles for 4 elements, 3.5 per element, with no stalls.
