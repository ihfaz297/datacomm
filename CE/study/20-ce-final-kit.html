Ch 7 Transmission media, from zero

Four sessions, about 40 minutes. No maths. Past finals ask 1 or 2 marks per question here, and they ask it almost every year, so these are the cheapest marks in the paper.

Session 1 of 4The big picture

  • A transmission medium is whatever carries the signal from sender to receiver: a copper wire, a glass fiber, or open air. Forouzan calls it "layer 0", under the physical layer.
  • Guided: the signal is held inside a physical path. Copper carries electric current; fiber carries light.
  • Unguided: electromagnetic waves through air or vacuum, with no wire. Anyone with a receiver in range can pick them up.
Write this

Guided vs unguided (2016-17 Q1e, 2 marks): Guided media give the signal a physical conduit from one device to another, and the signal stays inside it (twisted-pair, coaxial and fiber-optic cable). Unguided media carry electromagnetic waves without a physical conductor, through air or vacuum; the signal is broadcast and any device with a receiver can get it (radio waves, microwaves, infrared).

Major classes of guided media (TT1): twisted-pair cable, coaxial cable, fiber-optic cable.

Check 1. Name the three classes of guided media.
Twisted-pair, coaxial, fiber-optic.
Check 2. Your phone's Wi-Fi: guided or unguided?
Unguided. It is a radio signal through the air, no cable.
Check 3. Which of the three guided media carries light instead of current?
Fiber-optic.
break: 2 minutes

Session 2 of 4Copper: twisted pair and coax

Twisting swaps which wire is closer to the noise, so both get the same noise and the difference cancels it.
  • Twisted pair: two insulated copper wires twisted together. One carries the signal, the other is the reference, and the receiver reads the difference. Twisting makes noise land equally on both, so it cancels.
  • UTP (unshielded) has no shield: cheap, thin, the most common cable (phones, LANs). STP (shielded) wraps each pair in metal foil or braided mesh: less noise and crosstalk, but bulkier and dearer.
  • Coax: copper core, insulator, an outer metal braid, plastic cover. It carries higher frequencies than twisted pair, but the signal weakens faster, so it needs repeaters more often.
Write this

STP vs UTP (2019-20 Q4a, 1 mark): STP has a metal foil or braided-mesh shield around each pair, which blocks electromagnetic noise and crosstalk; UTP has no shield, so it is cheaper and lighter but picks up more noise.

Examples of each cable (2014-15 Q1b(i)):

  • Twisted pair: telephone lines, DSL, Ethernet LAN cable (10Base-T, 100Base-T, Cat 5 with an RJ-45 plug).
  • Coaxial: cable TV, traditional Ethernet (10Base2, 10Base5).
  • Fiber-optic: Internet backbone (SONET), cable-TV backbone, Fast Ethernet 100Base-FX.
Check 1. Why are the wires twisted?
So noise hits both wires equally. The receiver uses the difference between them, so equal noise cancels.
Check 2. Two jobs of coax's outer conductor?
Shield against noise, and second conductor that completes the circuit.
Check 3. Which is used for cable TV?
Coaxial cable (backbone may be fiber).
break: 2 minutes

Session 3 of 4Fiber optics

First the physics, which is the whole trick.

Light starts in the denser glass (shaded). The dashed line is the normal; the angle is measured from it.
Every time the light hits the boundary at more than the critical angle, it reflects back. It stays in the core for kilometres.
  • Light changes direction (bends) when it crosses into a material of different density. That is refraction.
  • If it hits the boundary at more than the critical angle, it does not cross at all. It bounces back: reflection.
  • A fiber is a glass core inside a less dense cladding. Light keeps reflecting off the boundary, so it stays in the core.
Write this

Refraction (TT1): the bending of light when it passes from one medium into another of different density. Reflection: when the angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle, the light does not pass into the second medium; it is turned back into the first.

Purpose of cladding (2016-17 Q4g, 2 marks): the cladding is glass or plastic of lower density than the core. Because of this difference, light that hits the core-cladding boundary (beyond the critical angle) is reflected back into the core, so the signal stays in the core and travels along the fiber instead of leaking out.

Advantages of fiber over twisted pair and coax (2016-17 Q4d):

  1. Higher bandwidth, so higher data rates.
  2. Less attenuation: a signal runs about 50 km without regeneration (copper needs repeaters every about 5 km).
  3. Immune to electromagnetic interference.
  4. Resists corrosive materials.
  5. Light weight.
  6. Hard to tap, so more secure.

Disadvantages of fiber (2015-16 Q4f, "write two"): (1) installation and maintenance need expertise; (2) light travels one way only, so two-way communication needs two fibers; (3) the cable and interfaces cost more.

Why do optical signals have a very short wavelength? (2016-17 Q1f): wavelength = propagation speed / frequency. Light has a very high frequency (around 1014 Hz), so its wavelength is tiny, under a micrometre (fibers use about 850, 1300 and 1550 nm). That very high frequency is what gives fiber its huge bandwidth, and glass has very low loss at these wavelengths.

Check 1. Which is denser, core or cladding?
The core. The less dense cladding is what makes light reflect back in.
Check 2. Light hits the boundary at less than the critical angle. What happens?
It refracts out into the cladding; the signal leaks away.
Check 3. Two disadvantages of fiber?
Needs expert installation and maintenance; one-way light so two fibers for duplex; expensive. Any two.
Bonus, only if done. Propagation modes: multimode step-index (many rays, sharp core edge, rays arrive at different times: distortion), multimode graded-index (density falls gradually from the centre, rays curve, less distortion), single-mode (very thin core, almost one straight ray, best for long distance).
break: 3 minutes

Session 4 of 4Wireless: how waves travel

Ground waves hug the earth; sky waves bounce off the ionosphere; line-of-sight goes straight, so the towers must see each other.
  • The frequency decides how a wave travels. Low frequencies hug the ground. Middle ones (2 to 30 MHz) go up and bounce off the ionosphere, so they reach far. High ones go in a straight line, so the antennas must see each other.
  • Radio waves (3 kHz to 1 GHz) spread in all directions (omnidirectional) and pass through walls: good for broadcasting.
  • Microwaves (1 to 300 GHz) go in a narrow beam (unidirectional): good for point-to-point links, satellite, cellular.
  • Infrared (300 GHz to 400 THz): short range, cannot pass walls, sunlight interferes. TV remotes.

Frequency range of VHF (2015-16 Q4d): 30 MHz to 300 MHz (sky and line-of-sight propagation; used for FM radio and VHF TV).

Propagation method for radio waves (2019-20 Q1g): radio waves are omnidirectional. At low frequencies they use ground propagation (they follow the curve of the earth); at 2 to 30 MHz they use sky propagation (reflected back down by the ionosphere), which lets them travel long distances, as in AM radio. Above 30 MHz they travel by line of sight.

Omnidirectional vs unidirectional: an omnidirectional antenna sends in all directions, so sender and receiver need not be aligned (radio waves). A unidirectional antenna sends a narrow beam, so the two antennas must be aligned (microwaves).

Check 1. FM radio is in which band?
VHF, 30 to 300 MHz.
Check 2. Why must microwave towers see each other?
Microwaves are line-of-sight and unidirectional; they travel in a straight narrow beam.
Check 3. What reflects sky waves back to earth?
The ionosphere.

Ch 8 Switching, from zero

Four sessions, about 50 minutes. Short on time? Do Session 3 first: the three-stage switch is one calculation and it came in three different finals with the same numbers.

Session 1 of 4Why switches, and the three kinds

  • Wiring every device to every other is far too many cables. So devices connect to switches, and switches pick the path.
  • Circuit switching is a phone call: a dedicated path is set up and reserved for the whole conversation, even during silence. Physical layer.
  • Datagram (packet switching) is posting letters: the message is cut into packets, each carries the full destination address and finds its own way. No setup. Packets can arrive out of order. Network layer (the Internet).
  • Virtual circuit (packet switching) is a train on a booked route: a setup phase chooses one path, then every packet follows it in order, but bandwidth is used only when there is data. Each packet carries a small local label, the VCI. Data-link layer.

Session 2 of 4Virtual circuits in detail, and datagram delay

The switch looks up (port 1, VCI 14), sends the frame out of port 3 and rewrites the label to 22.
  • A VCI (virtual-circuit identifier) is a small number in each frame. It only means something on one link, between two switches. Each switch swaps it using its table.
  • The table entries are created during setup and deleted at teardown.
Write this

What is a virtual-circuit network? Explain its phases. (2015-16 Q6c, 6 marks)

A virtual-circuit network is a cross between a circuit-switched network and a datagram network. Like circuit switching, it has setup and teardown phases and all packets of a message follow the same path. Like a datagram network, data is sent in packets, each packet carries an address in its header (a local VCI), and resources are allocated on demand. It is normally implemented in the data-link layer.

  1. Setup phase. The source sends a setup request frame. Each switch on the way makes an entry in its switching table (incoming port and VCI, outgoing port and VCI). The destination returns an acknowledgment frame, which completes the entries.
  2. Data-transfer phase. Every frame follows the same path. At each switch the table gives the outgoing port and the new VCI.
  3. Teardown phase. After the last frame the source sends a teardown request; the destination answers with a teardown confirmation; all switches delete the entries.

What is VCI? (2015-16 Q4g): a virtual-circuit identifier, a small number in a frame between two switches that identifies the virtual circuit. It has local scope only and changes from link to link.

Total delay in a datagram network (2015-16 Q4a), for a packet through two switches (three links):

Total delay = 3T + 3τ + w1 + w2
T = transmission time
τ = propagation time per link
w = waiting time in each switch

Three links, so the packet is transmitted three times and propagates three times, and it waits in two switches. A virtual circuit has 3T + 3τ + setup delay + teardown delay, and a circuit-switched network has no waiting time at the switches.

Check 1. The three phases of a virtual-circuit network?
Setup, data transfer, teardown.
Check 2. Is a VCI unique across the whole network?
No. It is local to one link and the switch rewrites it at every hop.
Check 3. A datagram crosses 3 switches (4 links). Total delay?
4T + 4τ + w1 + w2 + w3.

Session 3 of 4Inside a circuit switch: crossbar and three-stage switch

A 4 × 5 crossbar has 4 × 5 = 20 crosspoints.
  • A crossbar joins n inputs to m outputs with a grid of tiny switches, the crosspoints. An N × N crossbar needs N² crosspoints. For N = 100 that is 10,000, and most of them sit idle.
  • A three-stage switch uses several small crossbars in three columns to cut that number down.
The P8-12 switch: N = 100, n = 10, k = 4. Three of the ten outer crossbars are drawn.

Read the picture left to right:

  • Stage 1: N/n = 100/10 = 10 crossbars, each n × k = 10 × 4. Each takes 10 inputs but has only k = 4 ways out.
  • Stage 2: k = 4 crossbars, each (N/n) × (N/n) = 10 × 10. Each middle crossbar has one line from every first-stage crossbar.
  • Stage 3: 10 crossbars, each k × n = 4 × 10, the mirror of stage 1.
Crosspoints = 2kN + k(N/n)²
           = 2(4)(100) + 4(100/10)²

Write this

2016-17 Q3b (10 marks), 2019-20 Q2f, 2015-16 Q5c. "Three-stage space-division switch with N = 100. 10 crossbars at the first and third stages and 4 crossbars at the middle stage."

  1. Configuration diagram: draw the picture above. Label every box with its size (10×4, 10×10, 4×10) and write n = 10, k = 4, N/n = 10.
  2. Total crosspoints: stage 1: 10 × (10 × 4) = 400. Stage 2: 4 × (10 × 10) = 400. Stage 3: 10 × (4 × 10) = 400. Total = 1200.
  3. Simultaneous connections: each first-stage crossbar has only k = 4 outputs, so only 4 of its 10 inputs can be connected at once. 10 × 4 = 40.
  4. Single 100 × 100 crossbar: all 100 inputs can be connected at once (at the cost of 10,000 crosspoints).
  5. Blocking factor = 40 / 100 = 0.4. Only 40% of the inputs can be served at the same time.

For "Design a three-stage 100 × 100 switch with k = 4 and n = 10": give the three stage sizes above, the 1200 crosspoints, and note that this is 12% of the 10,000 a single crossbar needs; the price is blocking.

Blocking means a new connection is refused even though its output is free, because every path through the middle stage is busy. Fewer crosspoints buys you blocking.

Clos criterion (no blocking): n = √(N/2), k = 2n − 1, crosspoints ≥ 4N(√(2N) − 1).

Ch 12 Media access control, from zero

Five sessions, about an hour. The finals asked CDMA with Walsh codes, "define CSMA/CD", and "CSMA/CD vs CSMA/CA". ALOHA numbers are the textbook's favourite examples, so they are here too.

Session 1 of 5The problem, and the map

  • Many stations share one link: one cable (a bus) or the same air. If two talk at once, the signals mix and both are lost. That is a collision.
  • The media access control (MAC) sublayer of the data-link layer decides who may send when.
  • Three families: free-for-all and deal with collisions (random access), take turns by permission (controlled access), or split the channel by frequency, time or code (channelization).
Random access can have collisions; controlled access and channelization cannot.
Check 1. Is polling random access, controlled access or channelization?
Controlled access.
Check 2. Which family is CDMA in?
Channelization.
Check 3. Why can collisions happen in random access but not in controlled access?
In random access nobody asks permission, so two stations can send at once. In controlled access a station sends only when it has the right (its reservation, the poll, or the token).

Session 2 of 5ALOHA: just send

B sends at time t. A's end hits B's start; C's start hits B's end. Any frame starting between t − Tfr and t + Tfr collides with B.
  • Pure ALOHA: send whenever you have a frame. Wait for an acknowledgment. No ACK after a timeout (2 × Tp) means a collision: wait a random backoff and resend. Give up after Kmax = 15 tries.
  • Slotted ALOHA: time is cut into slots of one frame time, and you may start only at the beginning of a slot. That halves the danger window.
  • Vulnerable time = the window in which another frame would collide with yours.
Tfr = frame size / bit rate

Session 3 of 5CSMA and CSMA/CD: listen first

  • CSMA (carrier sense multiple access): listen before you talk. Sense the line; send only if it is idle.
  • Collisions still happen, because a signal takes time to travel. A far station may sense "idle" while your first bits are still on their way. Vulnerable time = propagation time Tp.
  • What to do when the line is busy (persistence):
    • 1-persistent: keep sensing, send the moment it goes idle. Most collisions. Ethernet uses it.
    • Nonpersistent: if busy, wait a random time, then sense again. Fewer collisions, but the line sits idle more.
    • p-persistent: slotted line; when idle, send with probability p, otherwise wait one slot and repeat. A balance of the two.
  • CSMA/CD (collision detection): also listen while you talk. If what you hear differs from what you send, there is a collision: stop, send a short jam signal so everyone knows, back off a random time, try again.

Write this: "Define the CSMA/CD process" (2014-15 Q6c)

CSMA/CD (carrier sense multiple access with collision detection) is a random-access method in which a station senses the medium before sending and keeps monitoring it while sending, so that it can detect a collision.

  1. The station senses the channel using a persistence method (1-persistent, nonpersistent or p-persistent) and starts sending when it is idle.
  2. While transmitting, it keeps listening. Transmission and collision detection run together.
  3. If no collision is detected until the whole frame is sent, the transmission is successful.
  4. If a collision is detected, it stops at once and sends a short jamming signal so all stations learn of it.
  5. It increments the attempt count K. If K exceeds the limit (15) it aborts; otherwise it waits a random backoff time (R × slot time, R from 0 to 2K − 1) and goes back to step 1.

The frame must be long enough that Tfr ≥ 2 × Tp, or the sender could finish before it hears of the collision. The channel's energy level shows its state: zero (idle), normal (one sender), abnormal, about double (collision). Draw the flow chart above.

Check 1. Why can CSMA not remove collisions?
Propagation delay: a station far away senses idle because the first bits have not reached it yet.
Check 2. 10 Mbps CSMA/CD, max propagation 25.6 µs. Minimum frame?
2 × 25.6 = 51.2 µs, times 10 Mbps = 512 bits = 64 bytes.
Check 3. Which persistence method has the most collisions?
1-persistent.

Session 4 of 5CSMA/CA: wireless avoids collisions

In wireless, a station cannot detect collisions:

  1. Its own transmission drowns out everything else, so it cannot listen while sending (doing so needs costly hardware).
  2. Hidden station problem: A and C both reach B but not each other, so they cannot hear each other's collision.
  3. Over distance the signal fades, so a collision at the far end may not be heard.

So it tries to avoid them, with three tools:

  • IFS (interframe space): even when the channel is idle, wait a short gap first, in case a far station has already started. A shorter IFS means higher priority.
  • Contention window: then wait a random number of time slots (the window doubles after each failure). If the channel turns busy, pause the timer, don't restart it.
  • Acknowledgment: the receiver sends an ACK. No ACK before the timeout means resend.

Plus the RTS / CTS handshake: the sender asks "request to send", the receiver answers "clear to send". Everyone else who hears either sets its NAV (network allocation vector), a timer that says "the channel is booked for this long, don't sense, don't send".

Controlled access (no collisions):

  • Reservation: before each round of data frames there is a reservation frame with one mini-slot per station. A station that wants to send marks its slot.
  • Polling: one primary station controls the others (secondaries). Poll: the primary asks each secondary "anything to send?". Select: the primary tells a secondary "get ready, I am sending to you".
  • Token passing: stations form a logical ring and pass a special frame, the token. Only the holder may send.

Channelization (no collisions):

  • FDMA: each station gets its own frequency band. TDMA: each gets its own time slot. CDMA: everybody sends at the same time on the whole band, and codes keep them apart.

CDMA: everyone talks at once

The book's picture: a big room where pairs talk at the same time, each pair in a different language. You only understand your own language; the rest is background. In CDMA the "language" is a chip code.

  • Each station gets a code of N chips, each chip +1 or −1. The codes are orthogonal: a code times itself (chip by chip, then add) gives N; times any other code gives 0.
  • Data rule: bit 1 → +1, bit 0 → −1, silent → 0.
  • Each station sends (data) × (its code). The channel carries the sum.
  • To hear station i: multiply the channel by station i's code, add, divide by N. +1 means 1, −1 means 0, 0 means silent.

Scrambling: B8ZS and HDB3 (20 minutes)

  • Start from AMI: a 0 is zero volts, and each 1 is a pulse whose polarity alternates (+, −, +, ...). No DC, good. But a long run of 0s is a flat line and the receiver's clock drifts.
  • Scrambling swaps a long run of zeros for a special pattern of pulses. The pattern contains a deliberate violation: a pulse with the same polarity as the one before, which normal AMI never does. The receiver spots the violation and turns the pattern back into zeros. No extra bits are added.
  • V (violation) = same polarity as the previous nonzero pulse. B (bipolar) = opposite polarity, the normal AMI rule. Both are relative to the pulse just before them.
The two rules 
B8ZS: 8 zeros -> 0 0 0 V B 0 V B

HDB3: 4 zeros -> 0 0 0 V  if ODD
                 B 0 0 V  if EVEN

"Odd" and "even" are the number of nonzero pulses since the last substitution. After each substitution the count restarts at 0 (even).

HDB3's choice keeps the number of pulses between violations even, so the positives and negatives balance and there is no DC.

Write this: "Explain Stop-and-Wait protocol" (2016-17 Q3c(i), 4 marks)

Stop-and-Wait is a connection-oriented data-link protocol with flow and error control. The sender sends one frame and waits for an acknowledgment before sending the next. Each frame carries a CRC; a corrupted frame is silently discarded. The sender keeps a copy of the frame and starts a timer; if the timer expires before an ACK arrives, it resends the frame. Frames are numbered with a 1-bit sequence number (0, 1), and the ACK carries the number of the next frame expected, so duplicates caused by lost ACKs can be detected and discarded.

ACK-3 and NAK-3 (2014-15 Q1b(v)): ACK-3 means frames up to 2 arrived safely and the receiver expects frame 3. NAK-3 means frame 3 was damaged or lost, so send frame 3 again.

What is TDM? Why is statistical TDM better than synchronous? F16F19
TDM is digital multiplexing where several connections share a link by taking turns in time; each gets slots in every frame. FDM vs FHSS, describe FHSS F15 / Define FHSS and how it spreads bandwidth F16 / FHSS vs DSSS F20 / DSSS name F15

FHSS (frequency-hopping spread spectrum): a pseudorandom code generator makes a k-bit pattern every hopping period; a frequency table turns it into a carrier frequency; a synthesizer makes that carrier, and the data modulates it. The signal hops among M frequencies, so it occupies about M × B: that is the spreading. It is hard to intercept or jam.

FDM vs FHSS: both spread signals over many frequency bands. In FDM each user keeps its own band all the time (many sources share a link). In FHSS one source jumps from band to band over time.

DSSS = direct sequence spread spectrum: each data bit is directly replaced by a sequence of n chips (a spreading code such as an 11-chip Barker sequence), which widens the bandwidth n times. FHSS changes the carrier frequency; DSSS keeps one carrier and replaces bits with chip codes.

Advantage of bit-oriented over byte-oriented protocols; define a bit-oriented protocol. F15 / Byte vs bit-oriented TT

Byte-oriented: the data are 8-bit characters, the flag is a byte, and any data byte that looks like the flag or the escape byte gets an extra ESC byte in front (byte stuffing). Bit-oriented: the data are a plain sequence of bits, the frame is marked by the flag 01111110, and after five consecutive 1s in the data the sender inserts a 0 (bit stuffing); the receiver removes it.

Advantage: bit stuffing adds a single bit instead of a whole byte, works for any kind of data (text, image, audio), and the flag can never appear inside the data, so after an error the receiver resynchronises at the next flag. Example: data 0111111101 is sent as 01111101101. A bit-oriented protocol (HDLC) treats the data section as a bit sequence delimited by flags.
